      The science

      What we stand on

      Every number in the calculator stands on one of these beliefs. Tap one to see the evidence.

      11 core beliefs

      Bottles never go above 8% carbsResearch

      What we believe

      A little carbohydrate helps fluid absorb, but stronger drinks slow fluid delivery. So the hotter the day, the weaker the bottle, and 8% is the ceiling.

      Why

      • A small amount of carbohydrate (1–3%) speeds fluid absorption, while drinks at 8% and above delay fluid delivery. Glucose + fructose blends soften that at higher concentrations.

        GSSI SSE #244, Baker (2023)

      • Fluid delivery was compromised once concentration went above 6%, and adding sodium didn't fix it.

        Jeukendrup et al., 2009

      • Repeated drinking of an 8% carb beverage during exercise significantly slowed gastric emptying; lower concentrations didn't.

        Murray et al., IJSNEM 1999

      What the app does

      Hot: 3% (fluid first). Moderate: 6%. Cold: 8%, and the app will not go higher. Our old 10% cold band was a tolerance habit, not evidence.

      Heat cuts how much carb you can useResearch

      What we believe

      In the heat your body burns less of the carbohydrate you take in, and your stomach empties more slowly.

      Why

      • Cyclists at 35°C vs 16°C oxidized less of the carbohydrate they drank and burned 25% more muscle glycogen.

        Jentjens et al., J Appl Physiol 2002

      • Runners in the heat used ~20% less ingested carbohydrate even with hydration kept normal.

        Mougin et al., 2025

      • In a 49°C (120°F) room, water left the stomach more slowly than at 18°C, tracking rising core temperature. At 35°C it slowed only when riders were dehydrated.

        Neufer et al., Eur J Appl Physiol 1989

      What the app does

      The "Heat: lower carb target ~15%" toggle. Off by default; the app nudges you in the Hot band.

      Above 60 g/hr you need glucose + fructoseResearch

      What we believe

      One kind of sugar tops out around 60 g/hr because the gut can only absorb it so fast. Going higher takes a glucose + fructose mix.

      Why

      • A single carbohydrate type tops out around 60 g/hr because its intestinal transporter saturates; glucose + fructose together can push oxidation to ~105 g/hr.

        Jeukendrup, Eur J Sport Sci 2008

      • ~60 g/hr for 2–3 hr efforts; ~90 g/hr for ultra-endurance, only with multiple transportable carbohydrates.

        Jeukendrup, Sports Med 2014GSSI SSE #106

      What the app does

      Red warning if a single-source product is used above 60 g/hr. Note above 90 g/hr. The library tags each product's carb type.

      Drink to your sweat rate, not a weather formulaGuideline

      What we believe

      Every rider sweats differently, so your measured sweat rate beats any formula. The goal is to keep body-water loss under 2%.

      Why

      • ACSM: drink to keep body-water loss under 2%, typically 400–800 mL/hr depending on sweat rate and size, with sodium around 0.5–0.7 g per liter. Sweat rates vary so much that a measured sweat test beats any formula.

        ACSM Position Stand, 2007

      • For events over ~4 hr, replace 50–80% of the sodium you lose in sweat.

        McCubbin, via CTS

      • Warmer air raises sweat rate about 0.04 L/hr per °C. Outdoor prediction also needs sun, wind, humidity, speed and body size, and is still only about ±0.45 L/hr, so weighing yourself on your own rides is sharper.

        Exp Physiol 2023J Appl Physiol 2024

      What the app does

      Fluid checks at 0.4 / 0.8 / 1.2 L/hr. A sweat-rate field in Profile, not a weather formula. "Feels-like" temperature is the one weather input because your weather app already folds in humidity and wind.

      Sodium follows sweatResearch

      What we believe

      Sweat saltiness is personal. Your hourly sodium loss is that saltiness times how much you sweat, so it rises when you sweat more.

      Why

      • Sweat sodium concentration is an individual trait (roughly 200–2,000 mg/L; ~1,000 is typical) and is what a sweat test reports. Hourly loss is that concentration times sweat volume, so it rises with heat, and concentration itself creeps up at higher sweat rates as ducts reabsorb less.

        Baker, 2017

      What the app does

      Profile stores mg/L; the plan multiplies by today's fluid, so a Hot-day fluid bump raises the sodium target with it. Drinking more plain water at a fixed sodium intake is the recipe for dilutional hyponatremia.

      Heat stress is WBGT, not feels-likeGuideline

      What we believe

      Heat stress depends on sun, wind and humidity as well as air temperature. WBGT counts all of them; the heat index assumes shade.

      Why

      • Wet bulb globe temperature is the heat-stress index used by ACSM, OSHA, the U.S. military and NWS. It combines temperature, humidity, wind and solar radiation; the heat index assumes shade.

        NWS Tulsa: WBGTNWS: WBGT vs heat index

      • Our estimate uses the same method as NWS's national WBGT forecast: the Dimiceli–Piltz black-globe equation with the Hunter–Minyard natural wet bulb, fed by Open-Meteo's hourly temperature, humidity, wind and solar radiation.

        NDFD WBGT algorithm (Boyer, NWS/MDL)

      • For U.S. locations the app first asks NWS for its own operational WBGT forecast (an NDFD element since June 2022) and uses that; elsewhere, or if NWS doesn't answer, it estimates with the same method. The finding says which one you got.

        NWS Service Change Notice 22-43

      What the app does

      When you use the weather lookup, the concentration band is set by average WBGT over your ride window (defaults: Hot ≥ 80°F, Cold < 60°F WBGT). Typed temperatures still use the feels-like thresholds. It's a forecast-based estimate, not a globe thermometer; shade, pavement and local wind move the real number.

      Caffeine goes where it works: the back halfResearch

      What we believe

      Caffeine should be peaking when fatigue sets in. On a long ride, that's the back half.

      Why

      • The ISSN position stand: 3–6 mg/kg taken about 60 minutes before it is needed improves endurance performance; doses above ~9 mg/kg add side effects, not benefit.

        Guest et al., 2021

      • Plasma caffeine peaks 30–60 minutes after a dose and has a half-life of roughly 5 hours, so a dose in the first hour still works in hour four but is fading. Late-exercise doses restore performance when fatigue sets in.

        Cox et al., 2002

      What the app does

      Short rides get the caffeinated gel first. Rides over 2.5 h get the first dose so its window covers the back half (about 90 minutes in on a 4-hour ride), then one more every ~2.5 h if the rider's per-ride cap allows. Nothing after the rider's cutoff time. The app never adds caffeine unless it is switched on in Profile, and never adjusts carbs or sodium for it.

      Small and often beats big gulpsResearch

      What we believe

      Small, regular drinks keep fluid and fuel moving through your stomach. Big gulps of strong drink slow it down.

      Why

      • Serial small drinks empty steadily; big boluses of strong solution do not.

        NASEM review

      What the app does

      Minimum gels per hour and even spacing. The exact "1 per hour" and "first gel at 20 min" are practice, not a paper.

      Medications get a flag, never a number changeGuideline

      What we believe

      Some common medications make heat harder to handle, but there's no evidence for a specific fueling change for any one drug.

      Why

      • Clinician guidance groups heat-sensitizing drugs by mechanism: SSRIs/SNRIs (impaired central thermoregulation, more sweating, dehydration and electrolyte risk), tricyclics and antipsychotics (reduced sweating), ADHD stimulants (more heat production), beta blockers (impaired skin blood flow), ACE inhibitors and GLP-1 agonists (blunted thirst), diuretics and lithium (fluid and electrolyte loss), first-generation antihistamines (reduced sweating).

        Americares / Harvard C-CHANGE provider guideNYC Health: Heat and MedicationsDrug Topics summary

      • The drug-name lookup uses the consolidated list from the Arizona Heat Preparedness Network's Heat and Medications sheet for providers (ADHS, Pima County, UArizona), which draws on CDC's clinician guidance.

        CDC: Heat and Medications — Guidance for Clinicians

      • We found no study that gives a specific fueling adjustment for any drug, so the app flags the mechanism and never changes a number.

        Our rule

      What the app does

      The app flags the mechanism and what to watch; it never changes a number because of a medication. Talk to your prescriber about training in heat.

      Where the evidence runs out, we say soOur rule

      What we believe

      Some numbers in the app are our judgment call, not a finding. We'd rather tell you than dress them up.

      Why

      • The exact temperature breakpoints (85°F, 65°F) are ours. Whether 8% is fine for you on a cold day is your gut. Per-person sodium needs vary several-fold.

      • Bottle roles are our call too: splitting fluid by cage count, and the warning when an electrolyte bottle passes ~1,500 mg sodium per liter. We found no study that sets how salty a bottle drunk with gels can be; the concern that a very salty drink plus a gel makes the gut contents hypertonic is reasoning, not a measured limit.

      What the app does

      Anything tagged Our rule is practice, not a paper. Use the journal to find what works for you.

      Coach's guidance: 75–85 g/hrOur rule

      What we believe

      Aim for 75 g/hr on easy rides and 85 g/hr on long rides with intervals.

      Why

      • From Bluebird Multisport: don't plan below 75 — the difference in recovery and how you feel is real.

      What the app does

      The intensity presets start at 75, 85 and 90 g/hr; you can change them in Profile.

      Disclaimer

      Bluebird Fuel is a planning tool, not medical advice. The numbers come from published sports-nutrition research (see the beliefs above) and from product labels you or we entered; your tolerance for carbs, fluid and sodium is individual and is yours to test in training before racing. Brands reformulate — check the label. If you have a medical condition affecting hydration, electrolytes or blood sugar, talk to a clinician.
