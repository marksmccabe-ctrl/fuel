    Units

    Temperature → bottle concentration cap

    Do-not-exceed caps, grams of carbs per 100 mL. Defaults follow the evidence: 3% when fluid delivery matters most (heat), 6% as the point above which fluid delivery drops, 8% as the ceiling where gastric emptying slows. Nothing above 8% is allowed. See the Science tab.

    Moderate
    The Plan tab will suggest bottles/hr from it.
    Whatever the gels don't cover goes into the bottles to 0.1 g. Rounding down can push bottles over the concentration cap — the app will flag it in red.

    Coach's guidance

    From Bluebird Multisport: aim for 75 g/hr on easy rides and 85 g/hr on long rides with intervals. Don't plan below 75 — the difference in recovery and how you feel is real.

    Disclaimer

    Bluebird Fuel is a planning tool, not medical advice.     What the engine believes, and why

    Every rule in the calculator maps to a finding below. Where the evidence runs out, the app says so instead of guessing. Sources are the papers and reviews we read; nothing here is padded.

    Bottle concentration caps: 3% / 6% / 8%

    A small amount of carbohydrate (1–3%) speeds fluid absorption, while drinks at 8% and above delay fluid delivery. Glucose + fructose blends soften that at higher concentrations. GSSI SSE #244, Baker (2023)

    Fluid delivery was compromised once concentration went above 6%, and adding sodium didn't fix it. Evans et al., 2009

    Repeated drinking of an 8% carb beverage during exercise significantly slowed gastric emptying; lower concentrations didn't. IJSNEM, 1999

    → Hot: 3% (fluid first). Moderate: 6%. Cold: 8%, and the app will not go higher. Our old 10% cold band was a tolerance habit, not evidence.

    Heat lowers how much carb you can use

    Cyclists at 35°C vs 16°C oxidized less of the carbohydrate they drank and burned 25% more muscle glycogen. Jentjens et al., J Appl Physiol 2002

    Runners in the heat used ~20% less ingested carbohydrate even with hydration kept normal. Mougin et al., 2025

    Water emptied from the stomach more slowly in a hot environment, likely from reduced gut blood flow. Fluid Replacement and Heat Stress (NASEM)

    → The "Heat: lower carb target ~15%" toggle. Off by default; the app nudges you in the Hot band.

    How many carbs per hour, and of what kind

    A single carbohydrate type tops out around 60 g/hr because its intestinal transporter saturates; glucose + fructose together can push oxidation to ~105 g/hr. Jeukendrup, Eur J Sport Sci 2008

    ~60 g/hr for 2–3 hr efforts; ~90 g/hr for ultra-endurance, only with multiple transportable carbohydrates. Jeukendrup, Sports Med 2014 · GSSI SSE #106

    → Red warning if a single-source product is used above 60 g/hr. Note above 90 g/hr. The library tags each product's carb type.

    Fluid and sodium

    ACSM: drink to keep body-water loss under 2%, typically 400–800 mL/hr depending on sweat rate and size, with sodium around 0.5–0.7 g per liter. Sweat rates vary so much that a measured sweat test beats any formula. ACSM Position Stand, 2007

    For events over ~4 hr, replace 50–80% of the sodium you lose in sweat. McCubbin, via CTS

    Warmer air raises sweat rate ~0.04 L/hr per °C, but real prediction needs wind, sun and humidity that weather apps don't reliably give. Exp Physiol 2023 · J Appl Physiol 2024

    → Fluid checks at 0.4 / 0.8 / 1.2 L/hr. A measured sweat-rate field in Settings, not a weather formula. "Feels-like" temperature is the one weather input because your weather app already folds in humidity and wind.

    Timing

    Serial small drinks empty steadily; big boluses of strong solution do not. NASEM review

    → Minimum gels per hour and even spacing. The exact "1 per hour" and "first gel at 20 min" are practice, not a paper.

    What we don't have evidence for

    The exact temperature breakpoints (85°F, 65°F) are ours. Whether 8% is fine for you on a cold day is your gut. Per-person sodium needs vary several-fold. Use the journal.
